    I

    Cloch

    The stone, 4th to 7th centuries

    The oldest writing in Irish is cut into the edge of a stone. Ogham records each sound as strokes and notches on either side of an edge, and it is read from the bottom up.

    Around 400 inscriptions survive, most of them from the 5th and 6th centuries. Nearly all are names: who someone was, and whose son. Most are found in an arc running from County Kerry to south Wales.

    The word that turns up most often is MAQI, ‘son of’. In modern Irish it is mac, the Mac in so many surnames.

    Carve a name in the stone

    Classical ogham has no P, J, K, V, W, X or Y, so those are swapped for the nearest letter.

    II

    Lámhscríbhinn

    The manuscript, 5th to 17th centuries

    Christianity brought the Latin alphabet in the 5th century. In the monasteries, scribes began jotting Irish in the margins of their Latin books.

    Those 8th- and 9th-century glosses are the bedrock of what we know about Old Irish. Out of them grew one of the oldest vernacular literatures in western Europe.

    The language crosses the sea

    From the 5th century, settlers from the north-east of Ireland carried their language to Scotland and the Isle of Man. Scottish Gaelic and Manx both grew from that stem.

    III

    Cúlú

    The retreat, 1366 to 1911

    For a thousand years Irish was the language of almost the whole island, Norman settlers included. The English conquest changed who ruled, who owned the land and which language got you on in life.

    Each knot in the thread is a date, tagged with what happened.

    1. 1366, Statutes of Kilkenny: English settlers are forbidden to speak Irish. The law itself is proof that they did.
    2. 1537, Act for English Order, Habit and Language: Henry VIII orders the Irish to take up English speech, dress and customs.
    3. 1607, Flight of the Earls: the last great Gaelic lords of Ulster leave for exile, and the professional poets lose their patrons.
    4. 1695, Penal Laws: the Catholic majority is shut out of land, education and office. English becomes the language of getting on.
    5. 1831, National Schools: state primary education is taught through English.
    6. 1845, An Gorta Mór, the Great Famine: by 1852 around a million people have died and well over a million more have emigrated. It strikes hardest in the west, where Irish was most widely spoken.

    The stones are censuses

    In 1851 the census asked for the first time who spoke Irish. Each stone on this avenue is a census, and its height is the number of people who said they could speak it in the 26 counties of today’s Republic.

    In 1861 that was 1,077,087 people, or 24.5%. By 1911 only 553,717 remained, 17.6%. The thickness of the thread follows the same figure.

    Gaps in the row are years without a census, such as 1921, or censuses that did not ask about language.

    IV

    Athbheochan

    The revival, 1893 to 2022

    By the late 19th century Irish was the language of the poor, rural west. A cultural movement set out to bring it back into public life, and the new state took it into the classroom.

    Each post along the avenue marks a date, between the censuses.

    1. 1893, Conradh na Gaeilge: Douglas Hyde and Eoin MacNeill found the Gaelic League to keep Irish alive as a spoken language.
    2. 1922, Irish Free State: Irish becomes the ‘national language’ and a compulsory subject in primary school.
    3. 1937, Bunreacht na hÉireann: Article 8 of the Constitution makes Irish the first official language.
    4. 1972, Raidió na Gaeltachta: Irish-language national radio goes on air.
    5. 1996, Teilifís na Gaeilge, now TG4. The same year, the census starts asking how often people speak Irish.
    6. 2003, Official Languages Act: sets out how public bodies must provide services in Irish.
    7. 2007, Irish becomes an official language of the European Union.
    8. 2021, the Act is amended: by 2030, one in five new recruits to the public service must be competent in Irish.
    9. 2022, full EU status from 1 January. In December, a new law gives Irish official status in Northern Ireland.

    The rise comes from school

    The last stone is the tallest in the row: 1,873,997 people. Nearly all of them learned their Irish in the classroom. Until 1996 the census only asked whether people could speak it, not how much they actually used it.

    VI

    Gaeltacht

    Where it is the language of the street

    The Gaeltacht is made up of the areas the State recognises as Irish-speaking. They are spread across seven counties, most of them along the west coast.

    106,220 people live there. 65,156 can speak Irish and 20,261 speak it daily outside education, down from 23,175 in 2011.

    On the map each pillar is one area. Its height shows daily speakers in 2022, and the thin cross above it marks the 2011 level. Positions are approximate.

    Beyond the Gaeltacht

    By county, Galway County (50%) and Clare (47%) have the highest share of people who say they can speak Irish. Dublin City (33%) and Louth (35%) have the lowest.

    In An Bun Beag in Donegal and An Cheathrú Rua in Galway, 69% of people speak it every day.

    In Northern Ireland, the 2021 census counted 228,600 people with some ability in Irish, or 12.4%. For around 6,000 of them it is their main language.

    On the map, the darker counties are above the national figure of 40%. Every county in the Republic is between 33% and 50%.

    VII

    Focail

    Words, the language from the inside

    Irish puts the verb first. Chonaic mé an fharraige is, word for word, ‘saw I the sea’.

    There is no single word for ‘yes’ or ‘no’. You answer by repeating the verb: An dtuigeann tú? ‘Do you understand?’ Tuigim. ‘I understand.’

    To say you speak a language, you say it is at you: Tá Gaeilge agam, ‘Irish is at me’.

    The first letter changes

    Depending on the word in front of it, a noun’s first consonant is softened (séimhiú, lenition) or covered by another (urú, eclipsis). Try it.

    Words English took from Irish and Gaelic

      Sluagh-ghairm and clann came into English through Scottish Gaelic, itself a daughter of medieval Irish. And craic went the other way: it is the English ‘crack’, respelt in Irish.
